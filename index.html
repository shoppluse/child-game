Prologue
3:47 AM — You Are Awake
Bengaluru · September 14, 2026
Live story — thriller ~18 min read 6 chapters

Your phone wakes you at 3:47 AM.

Not an alarm. Not a call. A text from a number you don't recognise — eleven digits, no country code, no name. The screen glows in the dark of your flat in Koramangala. Your ceiling fan hums. Somewhere outside, a dog barks twice and stops.

The message reads:

SMS · Unknown sender
He told us you'd delete this.
Don't.
Your brother didn't fall.

You sit up. Your mouth is dry.

Your brother Arjun died eight months ago. The police report said he fell from the terrace of his apartment building in Whitefield. Sixth floor. The investigation lasted eleven days and was closed with a single word: accidental.

You believed them. You had no reason not to.

You stare at the message until the screen dims. Then you type back: Who is this?

Three dots appear. Then they vanish. No reply comes.

You don't sleep again.

Chapter 1 of 6
Chapter 1
The Man in the Parking Lot
Day 1 · 9:12 AM

You go to the building. Arjun's building. You tell yourself it's just to look. Just to stand where he stood.

The security guard at the gate is new — a young man who wasn't there eight months ago. He lets you sign in without asking why. The visitor log has your name in it already. You didn't write it.

You ask him about this. He looks at the book. He looks at you. He says, quietly, "Sir wrote your name last week. Said his sister would come."

Sir. Arjun. Last week. Eight months after he died.

You take the lift to the sixth floor. The door to his flat is sealed with police tape that has yellowed and curled at the edges. Nobody has touched this place. And yet, slid under the door, there is an envelope. Cream-coloured. No stamp. Your name written on the front in handwriting you would know anywhere.

Your brother's handwriting.

Note — handwritten
"Priya. Don't go to the police. They are the problem. Go to the parking lot on level B2. White Honda City, KA-03-MF-4421. The man inside is the only one who knows everything. Tell him I sent you. He will be afraid. That's okay. So am I."

Your hands are shaking when you reach B2. The car is there. The engine is off. The windows are fogged. You tap on the glass and a man inside flinches so hard he hits the steering wheel. He rolls down the window two inches and stares at you with red, hollow eyes. He looks like he hasn't slept in weeks. He looks like a man who has been waiting for something terrible to find him.

"Arjun sent me," you say.

He closes his eyes. He says, almost to himself: "Then it's already too late."

He unlocks the door.

Chapter 2 of 6
Chapter 2
The Witness
Day 1 · 9:40 AM · Level B2

His name is Vikram Nair. He was Arjun's colleague at a mid-sized data analytics firm called Prism Solutions, on the 11th floor of a glass tower near Outer Ring Road. He speaks in a low, careful voice, like someone who has rehearsed this conversation many times and is still terrified to have it.

He tells you that six weeks before Arjun died, the two of them discovered something inside the company's internal data pipeline. Prism Solutions ran analytics contracts for three state government departments — health, infrastructure, land records. Vikram had found a routine data irregularity while auditing a transport ministry contract. He had brought it to Arjun. Arjun had pulled the thread.

What they found beneath it was not routine.

Vikram's account — verbatim
"Land records. Hundreds of them. Being modified in the system — small changes, parcels being reclassified — right before contracts were awarded. The beneficiaries were different shell companies but the ownership traced back to two names. One was a minister. The other was a name we both recognised from the news. A man people describe as untouchable."

Arjun had made copies of everything. He had given one copy to Vikram and kept one himself. He had told Vikram to hide it, tell no one, and wait for him to figure out what to do next.

Three weeks later, Arjun was dead.

"The day after the funeral," Vikram says, "two men came to my flat. Plain clothes. They didn't show badges. They told me Arjun had been under investigation for financial misconduct. They said if I knew anything about files he had taken from work, I should return them quietly. For my own good. That was the phrase — your own good."

"Did you?" you ask. "Return them?"

He shakes his head. He reaches under the driver's seat and pulls out a small hard drive in a ziplock bag. He holds it out to you like it weighs a hundred kilograms.

"Arjun made me promise," he says. "He said: if anything happens to me, give this to my sister. Only her. No one else."

You take it. Your palm is sweating. The drive is cold.

"They're watching me," Vikram says. "They've been watching me for months. I think they're watching you now too."

You look in the side mirror. At the far end of the parking level, a man in a grey shirt is leaning against a pillar. He is looking at his phone. He has been there since you got in the car.

Chapter 3 of 6
Chapter 3
Inside the Drive
Day 2 · 11 PM · Your flat

You spend eighteen hours finding a laptop that has never been connected to your accounts, buying it with cash at a shop in Jayanagar, and carrying it home on a different route each time you double back to check if you're being followed.

You are not sure if you're being followed. That uncertainty is its own form of terror.

The drive is password-protected. You try Arjun's birthday, your mother's name, the name of the street you grew up on. Nothing opens it. Then you remember: Arjun used to hide things in plain sight. You type the Wi-Fi password from the flat you shared ten years ago. The one he always said was too obvious. Priya1994koramangala.

The drive opens.

Inside: 847 files. Spreadsheets. PDFs. Screenshots of internal dashboards. A folder labelled VOICES containing forty-three audio recordings. And a video file, dated the night before he died, titled simply: for you.

You press play.

Arjun is sitting at his desk. He looks thin. His eyes are very still. He speaks directly to the camera with the quiet, controlled voice of someone who has already made peace with something.

Video log · Arjun Mehta · September 13, 2025 · 11:58 PM
"Priya. If you're watching this, you already know what happened to me was not an accident. I need you to understand something: I am not asking you to do anything dangerous. I am asking you to survive. Take this drive. Don't open it on any device connected to you. Find the journalist — her name is in the CONTACTS folder. She already knows half of this story. She's been waiting for the second half for eight months. Give her the drive. Then disappear for a while. Go to Amma's house. Stay there. Don't trust anyone who comes to you first. Not the police. Not anyone from my office. Not anyone who seems too helpful too fast."

He pauses. He looks off-camera for a moment, like he heard something. Then he looks back.

"I'm sorry I didn't tell you any of this while I was alive. I was trying to protect you. I understand now that I was wrong. The only thing that actually protects you is the truth — getting it out, making it too big to bury."

He leans close to the camera. His voice drops.

"One more thing. The man who gave the order — he has a name. It's in the last file in the VERIFIED folder. I want you to know: he is not untouchable. Nobody is. I almost believed they were. That was my mistake. Don't make it yours."

The video ends.

You sit in the dark of your flat for a long time. Outside, the city hums. A generator somewhere starts and stops. Your phone lights up — a number you don't recognise. You let it ring. It rings nine times. It stops.

Then a text arrives.

SMS · Unknown sender
We know you have the drive.
You have 24 hours to return it and forget everything.
After that, we stop asking nicely.
Chapter 4 of 6
Chapter 4
The Journalist
Day 3 · 7:15 AM

Her name is Sana Mirza. She writes for a publication that nobody powerful reads until the day they print something those people don't want printed. You find her contact in the CONTACTS folder on the drive — just an email address and a single sentence Arjun had written: She is careful. She is honest. She is not afraid of them. Trust her.

You write to her from a temporary email address you create at an internet cafe in Sadashivanagar. You say only: I am Arjun Mehta's sister. I have what he was going to give you. I need to meet in person.

She replies in four minutes. She names a coffee shop. She says: Come alone. If you feel followed, walk past and keep walking. I'll find you.

The coffee shop is small and loud. You arrive ten minutes early. You order nothing. At 7:15, a woman sits across from you without asking. She is in her mid-thirties, wearing the kind of tired that comes from years of doing something that matters. She looks at you the way a doctor looks at a patient — careful, assessing, already thinking three steps ahead.

"You look exactly like him," she says. It is not a pleasantry. It is an observation.

You slide the drive across the table under your palm. She doesn't look at it. She looks at you.

"Before I take that," she says, "I need you to understand something. Once this story is published, there is no undoing it. The men on that drive have resources. They have lawyers. They have people who will come after the people around you. Your mother. Friends. Anyone they can use as pressure. I need to know you have thought about that."

"I've thought about it," you say.

"And?"

You think of Arjun on the video, leaning toward the camera in the last minutes of his life, telling you that nobody is untouchable. You think of a parking lot. A drive hidden under a seat for eight months. A man who waited because a dead man had asked him to.

"He spent the last six weeks of his life trying to make sure this reached you," you say. "I'm not going to be the reason it didn't."

She picks up the drive.

What happens next moves faster than you expect. Sana's team works for eleven days. They verify every file, cross-reference every land record, authenticate every audio clip. They bring in a lawyer. They share the material with two international press partners for simultaneous publication.

On the morning of the twelfth day, you wake up to your phone flooding with notifications. The story is everywhere. Names. Numbers. Shell companies laid bare like dissected anatomy. The minister's office issues a denial that convinces nobody. Prism Solutions' CEO calls a press conference and says nothing in a great many words.

By noon, two arrests have been made.

By evening, the name in the last file of the VERIFIED folder — the name Arjun had saved for last, the name of the man who gave the order — is trending on every platform.

Your phone rings. This time it's a number you know. It's your mother. She is crying. You realise, with a jolt, that they are not the usual tears.

"They're saying on the news," she says. "They're saying what happened to Arjun — they're saying it wasn't an accident."

"I know, Amma," you say. "I know."

Chapter 5 of 6
Chapter 5 — Epilogue
What He Left Behind
Day 47

The trial takes months. You are called as a witness once. You sit in a courtroom that smells of old wood and old files and you answer questions in a steady voice that surprises you.

Vikram moves cities. He sends you one message: Still alive. Thank you.

You go back to the building one last time. Not to the parking lot. To the sixth floor. The police tape is gone. The flat has been cleared out. There is nothing inside except pale rectangles on the wall where Arjun's shelves used to be and the faint smell of him — coffee, old books, the particular soap he'd used since college.

You stand there for a few minutes. You don't cry. You already did that, alone, on the night you watched the video. You cried until there was nothing left and then you felt something settle in you like sediment after a flood — a quietness that is not peace exactly, but is not despair either.

It is the feeling of having done the one thing you could do.

On your way out, you notice something you missed the first time — scratched into the doorframe at eye level, in the small, neat hand he'd had since childhood:

Scratched into the doorframe — Arjun's handwriting
"Truth is not brave.
Silence is the coward.
— A.M."

You photograph it with your phone. You send it to Sana. She publishes it the next morning, at the top of her follow-up piece, with no caption. None is needed.

That night you sleep through until morning for the first time in forty-seven days.

Outside your window, Bengaluru hums in the dark. Autorickshaws, generators, someone cooking at midnight. The ordinary machinery of a city that does not know and does not stop.

Your phone is quiet. No unknown numbers. No warnings.

For now, at least — the silence feels like yours.
